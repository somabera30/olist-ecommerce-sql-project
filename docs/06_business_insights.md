# 06 — Final Insights: Customer Retention at Olist

## 1. Business Problem

Olist has a large delivered customer base, but repeat purchasing is extremely low. Of the 93,358 delivered customers in this analysis, only approximately 2.75% made another distinct purchasing occasion.

The main retention bottleneck occurs between the first and second purchases. Converting more first-time buyers into repeat customers is therefore the primary opportunity identified by this analysis.

Improving this conversion could help Olist generate additional purchases from its existing customer base, reduce its reliance on continuous customer acquisition, and potentially improve long-term customer value and profitability.

## 2. Retention Overview

Olist had 93,358 delivered customers, but only 2,565 made more than one distinct purchasing occasion. This represents a repeat customer rate of approximately 2.75%, indicating very limited repeat-purchasing behaviour.

### Purchase Progression

The purchasing distribution highlights where the main retention bottleneck occurs:

- **First purchase:** 93,358 customers.
- **Second purchase:** 2,565 customers, representing 2.75% of first-purchase customers.
- **Third purchase:** 202 customers, representing 7.88% of second-purchase customers.

Although the second-to-third purchase transition rate is higher, the number of customers reaching a third purchase remains small. The largest drop-off occurs between the first and second purchases, making the conversion of first-time buyers into repeat buyers the primary retention opportunity.

### Time to Second Purchase

Among the 2,565 customers who returned, the median time to their second purchasing occasion was 41 days, while the average was 88.86 days.

The difference between these measures indicates that some customers take considerably longer to return, pulling the average upward. The distribution also shows varied purchasing intervals: 45.9% returned within 30 days, while 35.4% returned after 90 days.

These findings show that returning customers do not follow a consistent purchasing timeline. However, this analysis describes the behaviour of customers who returned; it does not explain why the remaining customers did not make another purchase.

## 3. Understanding the First Purchase

To understand whether first-purchase characteristics help explain differences in repeat purchasing, we compared one-time and repeat customers across product category, basket size, order value, delivery duration, and review scores.

The analysis did not reveal any strong or consistent differences between the two customer groups.

- **Product category:** Repeat-purchase rates varied across categories, but no universal category pattern emerged.
- **Basket size:** Differences existed, but basket size did not show a consistent relationship with repeat purchasing.
- **Order value:** Repeat customers had a slightly lower average first-order value than one-time customers.
- **Delivery experience:** First-order delivery durations were very similar between the two groups.
- **Review scores:** Both groups had an average review score of approximately 4.2.

Overall, the first-purchase characteristics examined in this analysis did not provide a convincing explanation for the low repeat-purchase rate.

This does not establish that the first-purchase experience has no influence on retention. Rather, the available data did not reveal a clear relationship between these characteristics and subsequent purchasing behaviour.

## 4. Geographic Perspective

We examined repeat-purchase rates across customer states to identify potential geographic differences in retention.

The major customer states showed relatively similar repeat-purchase rates. For example, São Paulo recorded 2.86%, Rio de Janeiro 3.09%, and Minas Gerais 2.67%.

Some smaller states showed higher repeat-purchase rates, but their customer bases were considerably smaller. These rates should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as smaller customer counts can produce more variable results.

Overall, the analysis did not reveal a substantial geographic pattern that explains Olist's low repeat-purchase rate. Geographic differences may warrant further investigation, but the current findings do not identify a clear state-level retention opportunity.

## 5. Business Implications and Next Steps

### Key Business Implications

Olist's primary retention challenge is converting first-time buyers into repeat customers. With only approximately 2.75% of delivered customers making another purchasing occasion, improving this transition represents an opportunity to generate additional purchases from the existing customer base.

Our analysis of first-purchase characteristics and geographic differences did not reveal a clear explanation for the low repeat-purchase rate. Olist should therefore avoid assuming that a particular product category, order value, delivery experience, or customer location is responsible for the retention problem.

### Limitations

This analysis identifies purchasing patterns and associations but cannot establish the causes of customer behaviour.

The available dataset does not provide sufficient information about marketing campaigns, customer acquisition costs, competitor activity, or customers' purchasing activity outside Olist. Consequently, we cannot determine why customers do not return or quantify the financial impact of improving retention.

### Recommended Next Steps

Based on the findings, Olist should consider:

1. **Investigating customer behaviour:** Explore why first-time customers do not make another purchase, using additional customer feedback and behavioural data.
2. **Testing retention initiatives:** Experiment with targeted follow-up campaigns or second-purchase incentives for first-time buyers. Measure their impact on second-purchase conversion and incremental profitability.
3. **Evaluating market and competition:** Examine competitor offerings, pricing, loyalty initiatives, and broader market purchasing behaviour to identify potential external factors affecting retention.
4. **Measuring customer lifetime value (CLV):** Develop a CLV framework using appropriate revenue, margin, and cost data to understand long-term customer value and evaluate whether retention initiatives generate sustainable profitability.

The immediate priority should be improving first-to-second purchase conversion while testing which interventions generate sustainable and profitable repeat purchasing.
